Measuring the Ups and Downs: The Art of Levelling

Every map that shows a hill, every road that sheds rainwater properly and every bridge whose two halves meet neatly in the middle depends on one deceptively simple question: how high is this point compared with that one? Levelling is the name surveyors give to any process that answers it, whether they are fixing the elevation of a single point or the difference in elevation between two. Without it, mapping, engineering design and construction would be little more than guesswork.

The method a surveyor chooses depends mainly on the accuracy the job demands, although the terrain has its say too. Geometric levelling, carried out today with digital instruments, remains the gold standard. Trigonometric levelling is slightly less precise, yet it shines in mountainous country, where a conventional level would need endless set-ups. Whatever the technique, two kinds of trouble lurk in the background. Random errors scatter unpredictably, while systematic errors push results steadily in one direction, and the chief culprits among the latter are the curvature of the Earth, atmospheric refraction bending the line of sight, and careless calibration of the instruments.

The modern digital level owes its existence to microchips and image processing. At heart it is an automatic level fitted with a pendulum compensator, but instead of relying on a tired human eye squinting at graduations, it reads a bar-coded staff. A charge-coupled device captures an electronic image of the code, and the built-in software turns it into a height and a distance, storing everything for later processing on a computer.

When the stakes rise, as in deformation monitoring of dams or measuring mining subsidence, precise digital levelling takes over. Its staffs carry a bar code on a band of invar, an alloy whose remarkably low coefficient of expansion keeps it almost indifferent to temperature. The rules are strict. The backsight and foresight must be of nearly equal length, turning points must be solid, and the line of sight should stay at least half a metre above the ground to escape the worst refraction. Readings follow in rapid succession, typically in the order backsight, foresight, foresight, backsight, so that the air has no time to change its mind. Each line is run twice, forward and back, and this double-run levelling also exposes the sly effect of staffs slowly sinking into soft soil.

Trigonometric levelling takes a different route, calculating height differences from slope distances and zenith angles. Since targets can be placed at the same height above the ground, steep slopes no longer cut sights short, and they can stretch to a few hundred metres. The catch is that curvature and refraction grow with the square of the distance: barely three millimetres over 200 metres, but almost seventeen over half a kilometre. The elegant cure is to observe reciprocally and simultaneously, with two total stations facing each other from opposite ends of a line and measuring at the same moment, so that averaging the results makes most of these effects cancel out. The weak spot is the height of instrument, traditionally measured rather crudely with a folding ruler. One clever alternative places the instrument midway between two poles whose reflectors sit at identical heights, which means its own height never has to be measured at all. Modern total stations with dual-axis compensation and Automatic Target Recognition, which locates the centre of the prism without any focusing, make this a serious rival to spirit levelling, and they are already in every surveyor's kit.

GPS levelling adds a twist. Satellites deliver ellipsoidal heights, measured above a smooth mathematical surface, the reference ellipsoid. Engineers, however, need orthometric heights, measured along the plumb line from the geoid, which is the gravity equipotential surface that best matches mean sea level. The gap between these two surfaces is called the geoid height or geoidal undulation, while the angle between the ellipsoid normal and the true direction of gravity is known as the deviation of the vertical. Subtract the undulation from the ellipsoidal height and the orthometric height appears, provided a reliable geoid model exists for the area.

So how good is good enough? Accuracy standards set the allowable misclosure of a loop, which is a circuit that begins and ends on the same bench mark, as a constant multiplied by the square root of the distance in kilometres. Stricter constants apply to higher orders of work, although meeting the tolerance alone does not prove a given order has been reached. When precise digital levelling serves as the benchmark, ordinary digital levelling usually stays within about a millimetre per kilometre, trigonometric levelling within a few, and geoid-corrected GPS within a centimetre or so. A statistical t-test may even show these differences to be insignificant. The moral is reassuring: with care and patience, both the satellite and the total station can climb surprisingly close to the precision of the humble level.
