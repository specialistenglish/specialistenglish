Finding Your Place on the Planet

Imagine being stranded in the middle of the ocean, surrounded by nothing but water. How would you determine your exact location? For thousands of years, sailors faced precisely this challenge. The solution lies in two sets of invisible lines that encircle the planet like a vast geographical grid. Wherever a line of latitude intersects a line of longitude, there is one unique location on Earth.

Latitude indicates how far north or south of the equator a location lies. Imagine one line extending from your position to the very centre of the Earth, and another extending from the Earth's centre to the equator. The angle between these two lines represents your latitude. The equator is located at 0°, while the poles are at 90°. This is why the regions near the poles are described as the "high latitudes", while those closer to the equator are known as the "low latitudes". Because these lines run parallel to one another, <Blank answer="D"/>, they are called parallels. However, they gradually become shorter as they approach the poles, much like the rings of a spinning top.

Each degree of latitude is divided into 60 minutes. A useful fact for those interested in the geographic coordinate system is that one minute of latitude is exactly equal to one nautical mile, or approximately 1.85 kilometres. Each minute is further divided into 60 seconds, so a traditional geographical position might be expressed as 36° 15′ 32″ N. Today, however, <Blank answer="A"/>, producing a value such as 36.2597° N.

For centuries, navigators in the Northern Hemisphere relied on a particularly useful guide in the night sky: Polaris, the North Star. Polaris appears almost directly above the North Pole. If you were standing at the Pole, the star would be directly overhead, at an angle of 90° above the horizon, corresponding precisely to your latitude. At the equator, it would appear on the horizon, at 0°. At any location between these two extremes, <Blank answer="F"/>. Early sailors measured this angle using an instrument known as an astrolabe and later employed the more precise sextant.

Sailors in the Southern Hemisphere faced greater difficulties because there is no similarly bright and convenient star marking the south celestial pole. Instead, they used a method resembling a cosmic join-the-dots exercise. Longitude, by contrast, measures how far east or west a location lies from the prime meridian, an imaginary line that today passes through Greenwich in England. Unlike the equator, which is determined by the Earth's geography, this starting point is essentially arbitrary. At different periods in history, the prime meridian has passed through Rome, Paris, Philadelphia, and even Jerusalem. As one travels east or west, longitude eventually reaches 180°, on the opposite side of the planet, where the International Date Line is located. Lines of longitude, known as meridians, are all equal in length. However, rather than running parallel to one another, they converge at the poles. Consequently, a minute of longitude becomes progressively shorter as one moves away from the equator, eventually disappearing entirely at the poles.

This presented a significant challenge. To determine longitude, it is necessary to know two times simultaneously: the local time at your current location and the time at a distant reference point, such as your home port. Since the Earth completes one full rotation of 360° every 24 hours, <Blank answer="E"/>. In other words, every hour of difference between two clocks corresponds to 15° of longitude. Although this principle was relatively simple in theory, for much of history no clock was capable of maintaining sufficiently accurate time aboard a ship that was constantly rolling and pitching.

Although sailors had been determining their latitude using the stars since at least the third century BCE, longitude remained a major navigational problem until the mid-18th century. Before then, captains relied on a relatively inefficient method: they sailed north or south until they reached the desired latitude, then turned east or west and hoped to reach their destination. The problem was so serious that, in 1714, the British government offered a remarkable £20,000 reward to anyone who could determine longitude at sea to within half a degree. Astronomers proposed numerous ingenious solutions, but the eventual breakthrough came from a clockmaker named John Harrison. His first marine timekeeper, the H1, weighed more than 36 kilograms, yet his later models, the H4 and H5, were small enough to rest in the palm of your hand . In an unusual twist, Harrison was never officially declared the winner of the competition. In fact, no individual was ever formally awarded the prize. Nevertheless, his clocks revolutionised navigation. A ship could carry one clock set to Greenwich time and another set to local time, with the latter adjusted each day by observing the Sun. The difference between the two times could then be used to determine precisely how far east or west the ship had travelled.

Today, <Blank answer="C"/>. The Global Positioning System (GPS) relies on a network of satellites orbiting the Earth, each continuously transmitting its position and the exact time. A mobile phone or smartwatch can receive signals from several satellites simultaneously and use this information to determine its location, generally to within approximately nine metres. After thousands of years of experimentation, the contributions of numerous astronomers and navigators, and the persistence of one remarkably persevering clockmaker, determining one's location has become easier and more accurate than ever before.
