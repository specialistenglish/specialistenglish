Impact of the Datum Point Location on the Benchmark Standard Deviations

Precise geometric levelling has been used for more than 150 years to monitor sea-level changes, determine the geoid and quasi-geoid, and detect recent vertical movements of the Earth’s crust. It provides essential information about differences in elevation between points on the Earth’s surface. However, the accuracy of a levelling network depends not only on the quality of measurements but also on the mathematical adjustment process and the location of the datum point. Understanding these factors is important because even a small change in the network’s reference point can influence the estimated accuracy of benchmark heights.

A benchmark is a fixed point whose height is known or determined in relation to a vertical reference system. In a levelling network, several benchmarks are connected by levelling lines, along which differences in elevation are measured. These lines form loops that allow measurements to be checked and inconsistencies to be identified. Since measurements inevitably contain errors, the network must undergo a process known as least-squares adjustment. This mathematical procedure distributes residual errors throughout the network to obtain the most probable adjusted heights.

The accuracy of an adjusted benchmark height is expressed by its standard error, which indicates the expected uncertainty of the estimated elevation. This value depends on the standard error per unit weight and the corresponding diagonal element of the covariance matrix. The covariance matrix describes how uncertainties are distributed among the adjusted heights. It is calculated from the information matrix and the weights assigned to the measurements. The information matrix represents the connections between benchmarks: a starting point receives a coefficient of −1, an ending point receives +1, and all other points receive zero for that levelling line. When a benchmark is selected as the datum point, its height is fixed, which changes the mathematical structure of the adjustment.

Measurement weights are particularly important because they determine the relative influence of individual observations. A commonly used approach assigns weights inversely proportional to the length of each levelling line. This reflects the assumption that longer lines generally accumulate greater measurement uncertainty than shorter ones. Under this model, the standard error of a measured elevation difference is proportional to the square root of the levelling distance. However, the covariance matrix depends on the network configuration and the distribution of its measurements rather than directly on their actual accuracy. The standard error per unit weight, in contrast, reflects the residual discrepancies between observed and adjusted values and is influenced by measurement accuracy.

A levelling network therefore has several interconnected characteristics that influence its precision. Its configuration determines how benchmarks are connected, while its homogeneity depends on the relative weights of the measured elevation differences. The actual accuracy of the observations also affects the final results. Yet another factor is the location of the datum point, which may have a considerable influence on the standard errors of adjusted heights, even though it does not change the measured elevation differences between terminal benchmarks.

National precise levelling campaigns provide the fundamental data needed to establish and maintain reliable vertical reference systems. Bulgaria’s Third Levelling, carried out between 1975 and 1984, provides an example of how historical measurements can be used to examine the precision of a national network. Poland has also carried out four national precise levelling campaigns to establish and update its first-order vertical control network. The first campaign took place between 1926 and 1937 and established the initial national network. The second, conducted principally between 1953 and 1955, extended and updated the network following changes to Poland’s borders, although some accounts include earlier measurements from 1947 to 1950. The third campaign, carried out between 1974 and 1982, re-measured the network and covered approximately 17,015 km of levelling lines. The fourth campaign, conducted between 1997 and 2003, updated benchmark heights and provided information useful for investigating vertical ground movements. Poland’s third campaign is particularly relevant when compared with Bulgaria’s third levelling cycle because both involved measurements made during the 1970s and early 1980s. Their data remain valuable for examining levelling accuracy, benchmark-height uncertainty and movements of the Earth’s crust.

The Bulgarian network consists of 55 levelling lines forming 23 loops, with a total length of approximately 5,631 km. It contains 33 nodal benchmarks, which connect different parts of the network. Selecting different benchmarks as datum points produces the same adjusted elevation differences and the same mean adjustment error per unit weight, approximately 1.21 mm per square root of a kilometre. Nevertheless, the standard errors associated with the adjusted benchmark heights vary according to the datum point selected.

The most favourable results occur when the datum point is located near the geographical centre of the network. For example, choosing Knezha as the datum point produces an average benchmark standard error of approximately 10.89 mm. By comparison, using the official datum point in Varna, situated near the eastern edge of the network, results in an average standard error of 13.68 mm. The difference indicates that the average standard error is approximately 20% lower with Knezha as the datum point. A statistical comparison using a two-sample t-test, assuming equal variances, confirms that the difference between the two mean values is highly significant.

The geographical distribution of benchmarks helps explain this pattern. Areas with a higher concentration of benchmarks and stronger connections between levelling lines tend to achieve better precision. In contrast, sparsely connected regions may have greater uncertainty in their adjusted heights. The smallest average standard errors occur when the datum point is placed in Knezha, Borovan or Zlatitsa, while reference points in the southern and eastern parts of the network generally produce less favourable results. This suggests that selecting a datum point close to the network’s geographical centre can improve the overall distribution of precision.

Another important issue concerns the use of the standard error per unit weight as a general indicator of levelling accuracy. Although this value describes the overall quality of the adjustment, it does not necessarily predict the uncertainty of every benchmark height. For instance, the distance between Varna and the distant benchmark at Dospat is approximately 750 km. Applying the square-root distance model with a standard error per unit weight of 1.21 mm per square root of a kilometre gives an estimated standard error of 33.14 mm. The adjusted height, however, has a standard error of only 16.47 mm. This substantial difference demonstrates that the distance-based formula can overestimate uncertainty, particularly for benchmarks far from the datum point.

Such discrepancies suggest that the relationship between distance and uncertainty is more complex than a simple square-root model implies. The distribution of observations, the structure of the network and the statistical properties of measurement errors must also be considered. In particular, heteroscedasticity, which occurs when the variance of errors is not constant, may weaken the reliability of a model that assumes uncertainty increases uniformly with distance.

The choice of datum point is therefore an important element in levelling network design. Placing it near the geographical centre, especially in a region with a dense distribution of benchmarks, can reduce the average standard errors of adjusted heights and improve the uniformity of precision across the network. At the same time, the standard error per unit weight should not be treated as a sufficient measure of the accuracy of individual benchmark elevations. A more reliable assessment requires attention to the network’s geometry, measurement weights and actual observation errors. Together, these considerations provide a stronger basis for planning precise levelling operations and establishing dependable vertical reference systems.
